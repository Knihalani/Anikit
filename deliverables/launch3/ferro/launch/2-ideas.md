# 2. Ideas

## A. The toss gets higher
The pan flips what's in it, and every flip goes higher: an egg hops, a pancake turns over, a whole breakfast
lifts off, then everything hangs at the top of the flip — the moment a heavy pan could never give you. The last
thing to come down is the name, in iron.
- Literal test (delete "Ferro", delete "light cast iron"): "a pan flipping food higher and higher until it hangs in
  the air" — survives; it is about a gesture (tossing), not the casting or the weight number.
- Swap test: Le Creuset / Lodge / a classic skillet cannot honestly run "toss it one-handed"; their pans are the
  heavy ones. Passes.

## B. The scale
A kitchen scale: a classic skillet on one side, Ferro and a stack of ingredients on the other, balancing.
- Literal test: fails — it is the spec ("half the weight") drawn as a picture. Rejected.

## C. Hand-me-down relay
The same pan passed through five kitchens and five pairs of hands over a century.
- Literal test passes; swap test fails (any heirloom cast-iron brand); hands are also hard to render honestly.
  Rejected (and too close to the last film's century).

## D. (out there) The pan as a bell
Struck, an iron pan rings; the ring of this one never fades — the note sustains through the whole film, through
kitchens, years, while dishes are cooked in it.
- Literal: survives. Swap: generic "durable". Visual device weak (sound-only). Rejected; keep the iron ring as foley.

## E. Sky kitchen
Everything that's cooked in it floats up into the sky like balloons — "light". Too whimsical, and loses the iron.
Rejected.

## Pick: A — the toss gets higher
- **Device in one sentence:** every flip of the pan sends food higher than the last, until it all hangs in the air.
- **Escalation:** (1) an egg hops a few centimetres → (2) a pancake turns over at head height → (3) a full
  breakfast lifts off together, spinning → (4) the camera follows it up and everything **hangs** (hard stop,
  silence) → (5) it all drops back and lands in time → (6) the last toss throws up iron letters that land as FERRO.
- **Ending:** five cast-iron letters spin down out of the air and land on the counter beside the pan: FERRO. The
  CTA "Back it on Kickstarter" lands under them.
- **Why it won:** it is the only idea where the product *causes* a visible, escalating change (height), it's a
  gesture people recognise from cooking, and it fails the swap test against every heavy pan.

## F. The balance — two of ours, one of theirs  ← **pick**
A big equal-arm balance (a shop scale, brass beam on an iron post). A classic skillet slams onto one tray and throws the
lone Ferro on the other tray into the air. A second Ferro is lowered onto the first — and the beam swings, and settles
dead level. Two Ferros weigh one classic skillet: "half the weight", *shown* rather than written.
- Family: a **structural/physical proof device** (counterweight), not a gesture; a new world (concrete, brass, two irons,
  cool daylight) — different device and different world from A.
- Literal test: delete the name and mechanism → "two of one pan exactly balance one of another" — survives; it's a
  demonstration of a relation, not the product's name drawn.
- Swap test: no heavy-pan brand can run it; it only works for a pan at half the weight. Passes.
- Escalation: tipped the wrong way → slammed the other way, Ferro flung up → it hangs high and helpless → the second
  Ferro lands → the beam swings through level, oscillating → dead level (hard stop) → the name lands on the beam's
  fulcrum and the balance holds.
- Ending: five cast letters F-E-R-R-O drop onto the centre of the beam; because they land over the pivot the beam
  only shivers and holds level. CTA is stamped into the plinth.

## Rejected for pass 2
- G. Single-finger lift (a fingertip under the handle) — hands are hard to render honestly.
- H. Pan on a spring scale vs a classic one — same device as F with less drama, and needs numbers we don't have.
