# Kestrel — 6 s distributor ident · concept

## Proposition (one thing)
**Kestrel holds still on the films worth seeing.**
(An independent distributor watches hundreds of films and commits to a few. The kestrel is the bird that holds dead still in a moving sky until it sees the one thing worth diving for.)

## Facts (gathered; specific raw material)
1. Common kestrel, *Falco tinnunculus* — "tinnunculus" from Latin for *shrill/ringing*: its call is a sharp "kee-kee-kee".
2. Old English name: **windhover** (Hopkins, "The Windhover", 1877: "the achieve of, the mastery of the thing!").
3. When hovering, a kestrel's **head stays fixed in space to within millimetres** while body, wings and tail work against the wind (Videler et al., 1983).
4. The hover is really flying into the headwind at exactly the wind's speed: motionless by moving.
5. Kestrels see ultraviolet; vole urine trails reflect UV, so the bird reads the field like a printed map (Viitala et al., 1995).
6. It hunts by "hover and stoop": hold, fold the wings, drop, strike.
7. Hovers are typically 10–20 m up; a dive is a near-vertical drop, wings tucked into a dart.
8. Tail fanned and dipped as a rudder in the hover; the tail has one broad dark sub-terminal band.
9. Wingspan ~70–80 cm, mass ~200 g; long pointed falcon wings.
10. A dark "tear" stripe falls from the eye.
11. Chestnut back with black spots — the same colour as acetate film base.
12. Kestrels hunt over motorway verges: stillness above traffic.
13. Hovering is expensive in energy, so every hover is a decision.
14. A projector shows motion by **holding each frame perfectly still in the gate**, then pulling the film down in the dark (intermittent movement, Geneva/Maltese-cross drive).
15. 24 frames a second; a two-blade shutter flickers the light at 48 Hz.
16. 35 mm film: 4 perforations per frame, 16 frames per foot, 90 feet a minute.
17. The claw that pulls the film down engages the perforations: the projector's rattle *is* the sprocket count.
18. Edge codes are printed in the film margin, only readable when the strip is still.
19. Changeover **cue marks** — small dots flashed in the top-right corner, 8 s and 1 s before the end of a reel — tell the projectionist to start the next reel: "the film begins now".
20. The optical soundtrack sits between the perforations and the picture as a wiggling band of light.
21. Direct animation (Len Lye, Norman McLaren) draws images straight onto clear film with ink.
22. Halation: bright light bleeding red/orange through the film emulsion around highlights.
23. Gate weave: the projected image drifts a pixel or two because the film isn't perfectly registered.
24. Independent distributors buy at festivals after watching hundreds of films; they release a handful a year.
25. A distributor ident plays in the dark, right before the first frame of the film — the audience is at its stillest.

## The turn (de Bono provocation)
Obvious: *"a kestrel flies, then becomes the logo."*
**Po (reversal): the bird does not move — the world moves.** That is literally the kestrel's hover (fact 3) and literally a projector (fact 14): a frame held perfectly still in the gate while the film rushes through. Stepping stone → the principle "stillness inside motion" → the workable idea: the kestrel lives *on the film*, and its dive is the projector pulling the next frame down (21 + 22 + 23 + 26 combined).

**One sentence:** *A kestrel's hover shown as a projector's held frame — and its dive as the pull-down that brings the title into the gate.*
Swap test: replace "Kestrel" with any other distributor (A24, Neon, Mubi) and it collapses — the hover/gate rhyme only belongs to this name.

## Point of view — who made these images, with what, in what mood?
A projectionist, alone in the booth at 3 a.m. before an opening night, who inked a kestrel by hand straight onto clear 35 mm leader, one wingbeat per frame, and threads it through the projector to watch it hover. Mood: devoted, nocturnal, a little reverent. The images are *ink on celluloid seen by lamp light* — never vector-clean, never digital glow.

## Treatment — "THE GATE"
**Structure:** #12 single animated symbol fused with #2 the wait (tension → release). 6 s, 2 beats: hover (setup) → stoop (turn) → title held (payoff) → cue mark (button).

**World rules**
1. The only light source is the projector lamp behind the strip; everything is lit *through* film (multiply over one light field). Hot gate, falling spill.
2. Palette: booth black #070504 · lamp white #FFF2DA (gate) · amber spill #E9A75A · acetate chestnut #5A2E14 (film base, = the bird's back) · ink #0E0906. No other hues; no blue, ever.
3. Everything on the film is hand-inked: silhouettes, frame lines, letters. Ink boils slightly frame to frame (±1 px); no perfect vectors.
4. The bird's head never moves while hovering. Ever. Only the film and the wings move.
5. Motion is mechanical: the strip runs, decelerates, registers with a small claw-bump; the only fast move is the pull-down (gravity, ease-in, hard stop).
6. Type: the wordmark is a frame of film — clear letters in black ink, light passing through them. Wide-tracked grotesk caps; edge code in mono, tiny.
7. Camera: locked-off on the gate like a projectionist leaning in; one slow push-in; one bump on the impact. No orbit, no whip.
8. Texture: grain, halation, dust specks, gate weave, lamp flicker — all from the film world; no lens flares, no particles.
9. Sound comes from the machine and the bird: sprocket rattle (one tick per perforation), wind, silence at the stop, a drop, one warm chord, "kee-kee-kee".
10. Forbidden: gold, chrome, 3D, flares, curtains, reels, countdowns.

**Arc (24 fps, 120 bpm grid, beat = 0.5 s)**
- 0.00–2.80 HOVER. Frame 0: the strip already racing down through a blazing gate, perforations streaking; in the gate, an inked kestrel hovers, wings beating, head locked. Projector rattle + wind + a low cello. Slow push-in. The strip decelerates; frames above and below come into focus as a column of wingbeats, every eye on one line.
- 2.80–3.20 STOP. The film registers and stops. Silence. The bird folds its wings (the frame in the gate is the folded pose). Edge code "KESTREL" now legible in the margin.
- 3.20–3.50 STOOP. The pull-down: the strip drops one frame with accelerating ease; the bird falls out of the gate; whoosh.
- 3.50 STRIKE (@beat 8). The next frame slams into register: KESTREL, clear letters in black ink. Low impact + a warm D-major chord; camera bump.
- 3.50–6.00 PAYOFF held 2.5 s; light settles, grain breathes.
- ~5.45 BUTTON. The changeover cue mark flashes top-right for 4 frames + "kee-kee-kee" on a dry flute — the feature is about to start.

**One material pushed to the limit:** projected light through hand-inked celluloid (light field, halation, weave, flicker, ink boil).

**Re-watch reward:** the edge code reads KESTREL from frame 0 (blurred), and every frame on the strip has the bird's eye on the same line.

**Outside references:** Norman McLaren's *Begone Dull Care* (ink on film: take the hand-made line); Muybridge's plates (take the grid of phases); Hopkins' "The Windhover" (take the idea of mastery-as-stillness); a projection booth's changeover cue mark (take the button).
