# Art direction and concept development: from brief to unexpected idea

Scope: how creative directors, art directors and title/motion studios get from a brief to an idea nobody expected, then lock it into a look that stays consistent across a 10–30 s piece. Written for an agent that writes JSON specs for a renderer offering 2D vector and type, illustration, GLSL shaders, ray-traced 3D, particles and physics, and generated music.
Confidence tags: **[P]** primary source (the practitioner's own words or the study itself). **[S]** secondary summary of a primary source. **[H]** heuristic distilled here, not taken from a source.

## 1. Principles (why the process looks the way it does)

- **An idea is a new combination of known parts, so the raw material decides the ceiling.** James Webb Young's 1940s method has five steps: gather specific *and* general material, digest it, drop the problem and let it incubate, catch the "Eureka", then shape the idea against reality. Reported by fs.blog, Will Patrick and Alex Murrell. [S] Generic advice of the "be creative" kind fails because it skips step 1. An agent that hasn't collected 20+ specific facts about the subject is recombining clichés.
- **Reduce the idea to one image, then make it move.** Saul Bass aimed for "a simple, visual phrase that tells you what the picture is all about". For *The Man with the Golden Arm* he reduced the film to one jagged arm, and Preminger's response, "why not make it move?", produced the modern title sequence. [S, Wikipedia; Herrick Library interview excerpt] The single symbol is the brief. Motion is how it gets delivered.
- **The concept comes from inside the subject, not from the designer's toolkit.** Alan Williams: "if it doesn't perfectly align with the show's vision then it's wrong". He warns against pushing a show into your comfortable toolset. [P, Frame.io] Karin Fong (Imaginary Forces) judges a title by "does this main title truly describe this show and is [it] ideally unique to this show". [S, The Futur interview summary] The "swap test" in §5 follows from this.
- **Look through the eyes of the subject.** Kyle Cooper's *Se7en* titles work because the handmade type and the notebooks are made *by the killer*. Fincher "knew that he wanted it to be drawn by hand". Cooper went further and made it look as though the killer had made the film opticals himself. [P, Art of the Title] Point of view is a strong source of a look: ask *who made this image, with what tools, in what state of mind?*
- **Constraints start the work. Being constrained stops it.** John Hegarty, BBH co-founder, wants "the freedom of a tight brief" and says deadlines and constraints "force the creative process into reacting". He separates *constraints* from *constraining*, meaning anyone who says "don't go beyond". [P, D&AD 2026] Hegarty is also described as favouring briefs that are "very short, single-minded and in one way or another inspiring". [S, Campaign Asia / FS Forum]
- **Be sceptical of what you already know.** Dave Trott's "blind brainstorm": before any research, write down everything you believe about the product and market. It's "the only time you're actually in the same place as the consumer". His "backward telescope": the brand looks huge to its makers and tiny to everyone else. [S, Alex Murrell's summary of *Predatory Thinking*] For an agent, the brief's subject is *not* what the viewer cares about. The viewer's life is.
- **Permission to fail is built into the process.** Wieden+Kennedy's "Fail Harder" wall (about 100–120k pushpins) is Dan Wieden's statement that trial and error drives creativity. Nik Reed describes it as giving people "permission to be wrong" so that fearless ideas survive. [S, Dentsu-ho interview with Wieden; air.inc] In practice this means generating far more than you ship.
- **Most of the effect comes from creative quality.** Nielsen Catalina Solutions attributed about 47% of advertising sales lift (about 500 campaigns, 2016–17) to creative. A later version gives creative 49%, media 36% and brand 15%. Project Apollo in 2006 had creative at 65%. [S, Nielsen 2017; MarketingCharts] Effort spent on the idea is not decoration.
- **Feeling beats information.** Orlando Wood (System1, *Lemon*) finds that "right-brain" features win: people, narrative, dialogue, a strong sense of place, implicit communication such as glances. In System1's analysis, the more left-brain features (abstraction, decontextualised product, flat claims; Wood's full list is in *Lemon*) an ad has, the lower its star rating. Right-brain elements gave about 1.6× brand recall. [S, System1 blog; vendor data] Binet & Field (IPA Databank, 996 campaigns) find emotional campaigns drive larger long-term profit effects than rational ones. [S, IPA deck; Murrell summary]

## 2. Techniques and procedures (with numbers)

### 2.1 Brief → proposition (about 10 minutes)
1. Write the brief in **one sentence with one thing to say** (a single-minded proposition). If it needs "and", choose one. [S, BBH/Hegarty above]
2. **Blind brainstorm first** (Trott): list 15–20 things you believe about the subject *before* looking anything up. These are your clichés, and also the audience's starting point. Keep the list. It becomes the "obvious" list in §2.3. [S]
3. **Gather specifics** (Young step 1): 20–40 concrete facts. Materials, processes, numbers, places, sounds, historical oddities, how the thing is *made*, how it *fails*, and who loves it. Specific facts produce specific images. "Coffee is energising" produces nothing. "A barista pulls 900 shots a day and can hear when one is off" produces a film. [H, from Young]
4. **Move upstream** (Trott): if the stated problem is unsolvable, reframe it as the problem one step earlier that *can* be solved. [S, Pan Macmillan / Nile listing of *Predatory Thinking*] Honda "Grrr" (W+K, 2004) did this. "Sell Honda's new diesel" became "Honda's engineer hated diesel so much he redesigned it": *Hate something, change something*. [S, Nexus Studios; Wikipedia]

### 2.2 Volume: write many, expect the good ones late
- The copywriting norm credited to Luke Sullivan (*Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*) is **100 headlines/lines** per brief. Practitioners report that the obvious ideas fill roughly the first third and the unusual ones arrive later. [S, deathtoshitads; CoSchedule] Steve Portigal's "double hump": the first wave of ideas is the obvious one, and only after it runs out does a second, original wave come. [S]
- Agent procedure [H]: produce **≥30 one-line concepts** before choosing. Number them. Discard 1–10 automatically unless one survives the swap test (§5). Then shortlist 3, deliberately far apart from each other (see §2.5).

### 2.3 "Obvious first, then turn it"
Write the obvious execution in one line, then apply a *turn*. de Bono's provocation operators (written "Po: …") are a standard set: **escape** (remove something taken for granted), **reversal**, **exaggeration**, **distortion** and **wishful thinking**. The method treats the provocation as a stepping stone. You extract its principle and then move to a workable idea. [S, Wikipedia "Po"; Union College course PDF]
Worked examples, all real:
- Obvious: "car ad shows car driving." Escape the driving, and show the parts instead → Honda "Cog": a chain reaction built from a disassembled Accord. [S, Wikipedia] (Note: the often-quoted "606 takes" is debunked as a number on a slate.)
- Obvious: "beer ad shows people enjoying beer." Reverse the weakness. The slow pour becomes the virtue → Guinness "Surfer", "Good things come to those who wait" (AMV BBDO, dir. Glazer). The client's research was set aside. [S, Creative Salon; Creative Moment]
- Obvious: "kid can't wait for Christmas presents." Reverse the motive. He can't wait to *give* → John Lewis "The Long Wait" (adam&eve, 2011). [S, D&AD; ITV Media]
- Obvious: "search engine ad lists features." Escape the camera, and tell a love story entirely in search queries → Google "Parisian Love" (Creative Lab, 2010). [S, CNN Money; Computerworld]
- Obvious: "channel logo appears." Distort it: the logo is hidden in a real scene and lines up for less than 1 s → Channel 4 2004 idents (Brett Foraker / 4creative / MPC). [S, Wikipedia; Herts paper]

### 2.4 Forced connection and other angles
- **Random-word technique** (de Bono, as taught): pick an unrelated noun, list 5 connections to the problem however loose, develop the strongest. [S]
- **Attack from 5 angles** [H, synthesised from the sources above]: (1) the product's *process/material*, (2) the *user's* moment, (3) the *enemy* or problem, as with Grrr's hatred, (4) the *maker's* point of view, as with *Se7en*'s killer, (5) a *medium borrowed* from another world, as with Parisian Love's interface or Cog's Rube Goldberg machine. Write 5 concepts per angle.
- **Crossover** (Trott): new ideas are new reactions between existing ideas, so import a format from outside the category (an instruction manual, a weather map, a museum label, a sports broadcast). [S, davetrott.co.uk "Crossover Creativity"]
- **Go too far, then come back.** That is Karin Fong's work mantra. [S, Wix Studio] Push the idea until it breaks, then retreat one step.

### 2.5 From idea to medium and technique
- Choose the medium that **is** the idea, not the one that's available. Williams warns against forcing a show into your familiar toolset. [P, Frame.io] Fong distils "a central idea, theme or emotion" into a metaphor or symbol and looks for an "aha" moment. [S]
- Mapping [H] for an engine that offers several media:
  - The idea is about **making or mechanism** → physics/rigid bodies, ray-traced 3D with real materials (the Cog family).
  - The idea is about **a mind or a person's handwork** → illustration and hand-drawn type, with jitter and imperfection (the *Se7en* family).
  - The idea is about **energy, sound or abstraction** → GLSL shaders and particles driven by the music.
  - The idea is about **information or language** → kinetic type, where the words *are* the actors (the Parisian Love family).
  - **Never stack all media in one piece** because they are available. One dominant medium and at most one accent.
- Patrick Clair (Elastic, then Antibody) works in this order: moodboards that "evoke something related to the show" → style frames → a storyboard artist → "identifying an arc". The Westworld pitch took about 4–5 weeks. 3–5 studios typically pitch for a main title. [S, Frame.io; vodzilla; TheWrap] *True Detective*: "broken portraits" built from "broken landscapes", where the people and the place are the same image. [P, Frame.io]

### 2.6 What a good treatment contains
Synthesised from Clair's and Williams's process and from treatment guides. Williams writes the treatment "from day one". [P, Frame.io; S, cg-wire guide: 3–8 pages of prose emphasising visuals, emotion, turning points]
1. **Title + one-line idea** (the single image, Bass-style).
2. **Why**: the insight or truth it rests on (one paragraph).
3. **World rules**: palette (3–5 colours with roles), type (1–2 families, how type behaves), materials/texture, camera language (lens, height, movement verbs), lighting key, and what is *never* allowed. This is the art-bible core.
4. **Arc**: 3–6 beats with timecodes, plus the final image.
5. **Sound idea**: tempo/bpm, instrumentation, where the hit lands. Williams starts from a song. [P]
6. **References from outside the category**: films, art, objects. Each comes with a note on *what* to take (light, not composition, and so on).
7. **3–5 style frames** at final quality.

### 2.7 Style frames: how ADs judge them
- A style frame is a *finished-looking still* that also implies the motion to come. The test practitioners use: could an animator who has never spoken to the client open this frame and know what the finished piece should feel like? "If the still does not hold up as a still, adding movement will not save it." [S, artofstyleframe.com]
- Caroline Le's five questions (Motionographer): who is it for? who or what is the protagonist and what do they believe? what is the story (how does the protagonist change)? what tone, i.e. colour, composition, camera, hierarchy "used with restraint"? literal or metaphorical, and which tools? [P]
- AD checklist [H]: (a) one focal point, readable at thumbnail size; (b) value structure works in greyscale; (c) the palette follows the world rules; (d) type is *designed*, meaning set with intent for size, tracking, line breaks and placement, not dropped onto a centred safe area; (e) every frame in the set is plainly from the same world; (f) at least one frame you could not have predicted from the brief.

## 3. What separates studio/award work from amateur work

| Amateur | Studio/award |
|---|---|
| Starts in the software | Starts with research, a one-line idea and a treatment (Williams, Clair) |
| First idea shipped | 30–100 ideas, picked late (Sullivan, Portigal) |
| Medium chosen for what's available | Medium *is* the idea (Cog's real parts, *Se7en*'s hand type) |
| Style = effects | Style = world rules and point of view (who made this image?) |
| Category references (other ads) | Outside references: Cog's Rube Goldberg machine, Parisian Love's search box, Clair's landscapes |
| Literal | Single metaphor (Bass's arm, Clair's light bulb in *Halt and Catch Fire*) |
| Hedged, many messages | Single-minded; sacrifices everything else |
| Rendered flawlessly but generic | Handmade "life of its own" (Cooper on analog unpredictability) [P, Art of the Title] |
| Brand on top | Brand *is* the event (Channel 4's 4 forming in the scene) |

## 4. Anti-patterns
- **The swap failure**: the piece would still work with a competitor's name on it. This fails Fong's "unique to this show" test.
- **Toolkit-first**: picking "shader + particles + 3D" before the idea. Williams calls this forcing the show into your comfort zone.
- **Category mimicry**: references taken only from other motion reels and ads. The result looks like the median reel.
- **Too many ideas in one film**: two propositions means zero. Hegarty: single-minded.
- **Left-brain drift** (Wood): abstraction, disembodied type lists, product-on-gradient, no people or place, no implied relationships.
- **Unmotivated polish**: glow, chromatic aberration, bokeh and lens flares with no world-rule reason.
- **Darlings**: keeping a beautiful shot that doesn't serve the one idea. W+K's fail-harder culture assumes most work dies.
- **Constraint-free briefs**: "make something cool" yields the average. Invent constraints: one colour, one camera move, a single take, only type.

## 5. How an agent can check its own work
1. **One-sentence test**: can you state the idea in ≤12 words *without naming the technique*? ("A love story told only in search queries.")
2. **Swap test**: replace the brand or subject with a competitor. If it still works, the idea is not specific enough. Go back to §2.1 step 3.
3. **Obvious test**: is this idea in your own blind-brainstorm list (§2.1 step 2) or among concepts 1–10? If so, apply a Po operator.
4. **Point-of-view test**: answer "who made these images, with what, in what mood?" If the answer is "a motion designer, in After Effects", there is no point of view.
5. **Thumbnail/greyscale test** on 3 style frames: render at 160 px wide and in greyscale. Is there one focal point and a clear value hierarchy?
6. **World-rules audit**: list 8–12 rules (palette roles, type behaviour, camera verbs, material, forbidden things). Check every scene against them, and count violations.
7. **Outside-reference test**: at least 2 of your references come from outside motion design and advertising.
8. **Right-brain count** (Wood): does it have a character, a place, a relationship or glance, a story change? Aim for ≥2.
9. **Kill check**: name the best-looking shot that isn't serving the idea, and cut it.


## 6. Gaps against Anikit (TASTE)

- **The treatment and style-frame workflow already exists in pieces; name it.**
  - `still --at` renders a style frame and `sheet` the set; the skills only ask for a `concept.txt`.
  - A treatment (§2.6) with world rules plus 3–5 style frames, judged by §2.7, would put an AD's checkpoint before the full render. That also makes it the cheapest place to catch "boring".
- **World rules can now be declared (`"world": {"palette", "fonts"}`), and `critique` flags drift from them.** What's still missing is camera verbs and "never" rules, which only a person or an LLM critic can judge.
- *(was)* **World rules can't be declared, so they can't be checked.**
  - A spec has no place to say "palette roles, the type that's allowed, camera verbs, what's never allowed".
  - A `"world": {…}` block that `critique` audits (§5.6) would turn the art bible into feedback.
- **Point of view needs a hand.**
  - "Who made this image, with what?" needs media that can show a maker: hand-drawn line with pressure, scratched or etched type, print misregistration.
  - COMPONENTS (`brush`, `sketch`, `hatch`, `paper`, `cutout`) and SHADER (print filters) cover the start. Type with a hand (TYPE) is the biggest missing piece.
- **Volume is cheap for an agent, and the skills don't ask for it.**
  - 30+ one-line concepts and a de Bono turn cost seconds of text.
  - The director prompt asks for 3 directions; adding "≥30 lines, drop the first 10, turn the obvious one" is a process change, not an engine one.
