# Why the div-a…g pieces read as boring: a craft review

These are the seven diversity-run films the user rejected ("insanely boring", "AI slop typography"). The concepts were often decent: an azulejo as a design system, Arctic data drawn to scale, a power station as a line-up. What failed was execution, and it failed in the same ways across very different briefs. That makes these failures structural, and worth fixing in the engine and the skills rather than piece by piece. Contact sheets: `deliverables/*/contact-sheet.*` on `claude/epic-ramanujan-qe7x4w-v-div-{a..g}`.

## Seven recurring failures

1. **The typography was the default, not designed.**
   - What we saw:
     - one weight of a neutral grotesk set at a modest size;
     - centred stacks on the end cards;
     - no ligatures, kerning pairs or optical sizes;
     - no type that behaves like the subject.
   - Where: div-a (Inter Display over kraft paper), div-e (Instrument Serif, small, on navy), div-c (a condensed grotesk). Div-c is the strongest, but it is still a poster-template stack.
   - What studio work does instead: a face chosen for the world, with large scale jumps and type that *does* something (cut, stacked, interlocked, set on a path, set into the material). See Stranger Things, Se7en, and Pentagram's motion identities.
   - Gap: the engine bundles 3 faces. Text has no per-glyph material, path, variable axes or optical sizing.
2. **The same UI furniture appeared in every piece.**
   - What we saw: a tracked mono "02 / PROCESS" eyebrow, a pill label "01 · one module", a "LIVE" dot, corner metadata.
   - Where: a, c, d, g. It's the landing-page grammar again, and it makes four different briefs look made by one template.
   - In studio work, labels exist only when the world has them: a train timetable, a map legend, a lab notebook.
3. **The frames were sparse and slide-like.**
   - What we saw: one illustration and a headline in each frame, with plain grounds around them. A paused frame is a layout, not an image.
   - Where: the kraft paper in div-a, and the navy with one disc in div-e.
   - Award work has foreground, midground and background, texture, secondary motion, and light that models form.
4. **The illustration was clip-art.**
   - What we saw: generic vector icons (an apple, a leaf, a rocket, a whale, balloons) in flat fills with uniform strokes.
   - Where: div-a's fruit and beans; div-d's children's-book set, where the fox, whale and rocket each come from a different world.
   - What studio illustration has instead:
     - a consistent hand (line weight that varies, an implied pressure);
     - a shape language drawn from one reference;
     - texture (grain, misregistration, paper);
     - staging with overlap and depth.
5. **Each piece had one move, and every shot repeated it.**
   - What we saw: the azulejo field (g) in 8 of 15 seconds; the lightning bolt (c) in 9 of 15; the bar chart (e) growing for 12 s.
   - Studio work escalates: each beat changes scale, angle or rule, and there is a turn the viewer didn't predict.
6. **There was no camera.**
   - What we saw: almost every shot was a locked-off front view of a flat plane, with motion only inside the frame.
   - Even graphic work moves the camera in title design (*Game of Thrones*' motion-control flight) and in 2D studios (parallax, push-ins through layers, crash zooms, rack focus). With no camera language, everything reads as slides.
7. **There was no finish.**
   - What we saw: no grade, no halation or grain chosen for the world, flat values (e's navy mumble), and pure vector edges everywhere.
   - Finish is where "a full human design team spent ages on it" shows: texture at the edges, light on type, a deliberate grade.

## What the better moments had

- **div-c** at 1.5–4.5 s: huge condensed type that bleeds off the frame, hard colour flips on the beat, and a world rule (circuits as voltage).
- **div-g** at 3–7 s: the rule-driven tile field is genuine generative imagery, and it is dense.
- **div-e**'s area-true discs: data drawn honestly at scale, which is an idea and not decoration.

These all work by committing to a world rule and pushing it to the edge of the frame.

## What this asks of each domain (for TYPE, GRAPHIC, MOGRAPH, 3D, COMPONENTS, SOUND, SHADER)

- **TYPE:**
  - a far larger licensed (OFL) library, including display faces with character;
  - variable axes and optical sizes;
  - type on paths and type as material;
  - kinetic type treatments that behave like the subject.
- **GRAPHIC:** layout systems that aren't the landing-page grid; labels only when the world has them; scale jumps of 5–20×.
- **MOGRAPH:**
  - escalation across beats;
  - transitions that are ideas (match on shape, morph, continuous camera);
  - camera language for 2D (parallax, push through layers).
- **COMPONENTS (illustration):** one hand per piece (variable line, pressure, texture), and a shape language derived from a reference rather than clip-art primitives.
- **3D:** non-studio worlds; light that models form; materials from the subject.
- **SHADER:** generative imagery as worlds (not just backdrops), plus finish passes (grain, halation, print).
- **SOUND:** sound design from the subject's materials, and a score that turns when the picture turns.
