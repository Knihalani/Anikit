# Se7en main title — Kyle Cooper at R/GA (1995)

**Why it's in the set (classic): the most influential type-led title of its era. Scratched, jittering type and a staccato macro edit make the audience feel the killer's hand.**

## The idea
The titles show John Doe preparing his notebooks: the hands of the killer write, cut, bind and develop photos. "Fincher and I decided to use hand-drawn [type] mixed with Helvetica… because it was from the mind of the killer… wanting it to be like the killer did the film opticals himself" (Cooper, *source*: Art of the Title).

## Structure and pacing
- 128 s (*source*: Vimeo oEmbed of the Imaginary Forces upload).
- Fast, staccato edit by Angus Wall (*source*): many very short tabletop macro shots (razor blades shaving fingertips, needle and thread, the word "God" cut from a dollar bill, writing in notebooks) intercut with title cards. Median shot under 1.5 s (*est.*, from the "staccato" description and the number of shots implied by the storyboard set).
- No narrative climax; the tension is cumulative.

## Typography
- Hand-etched into black scratchboard, mixed with Helvetica, then "manipulated during the film transfer process to further smear and jitter it"; the transfer was "cut up and reassembled during post production to add a final layer of temporal distress" (*source*).
- Letters jitter frame to frame, double-print, and flash on and off; legibility is deliberately borderline ("would likely break several guild legibility rules", *source*).
- White, light grey or red scratched type on black (*est.*, from stills).

## Colour and light
Desaturated sepia and cold green-grey tabletop photography by Harris Savides, with hard backlight through notebook pages ("see through the pages and you see the shadows behind the page and the backlight", *source*). Pure black title cards between.

## Motion techniques
- Tabletop macro live action; inserts of found images and developed photos (*source*).
- Assembled mostly by hand as a traditional film optical (Pacific Title), keeping accidents: "fish hooks and razor blades going through the roll-up machine" (*source*).
- Leader, frame-line and film artefacts used as graphic material (*source*: "piecing together bits of leader and other film artifacts").

## Transitions
Hard cuts, flash frames and jump cuts, with type flickering on and off between shots (*source*: staccato edit; *est.* for flash frames from the description).

## Sound design and music
Coil/Danny Hyde's remix of Nine Inch Nails' "Closer" ("nail-on-chalkboard", *source*): an abrasive, industrial bed of distorted textures rather than a song structure. Edits and type flickers lock to its noise bursts (*est.*).

## What makes it world-class
1. The texture of the type is the character: every letter visibly comes from a disturbed hand, and the jitter is uneven and physical, not a uniform "wiggle" effect.
2. Macro tabletop shots with hard backlight: tactile, specific objects instead of generic stock.
3. The edit rhythm follows the sound design hit for hit, cumulative rather than building.
4. Analogue accidents (scratches, frame slips, leader) are kept as content, not as a filter.
5. Legibility is deliberately pushed, but credits remain readable within their short holds.

## What Anikit would need
`video`/`image` macro shots, `text` with per-character jitter (`animator` with random order), `transitions` (`cut`, `glitch` used sparingly), `post` (grain, contrast), and flash frames via short layers. GAPs: a hand-scratched emulsion look for type (stroke irregularity, smearing, double printing per frame); procedural film damage (leader, frame-line slip, scratches).

## Judge's checklist
- Does the type's texture and jitter come from a concept (who made it?), and is it irregular frame to frame rather than a looping wiggle?
- Is each credit readable for at least a few frames even when distressed?
- Do cuts and flickers lock to specific sound events?
- Are the objects shown specific and tactile at macro scale (no generic stock)?
- Are analogue artefacts used as content, varied rather than a uniform overlay?
