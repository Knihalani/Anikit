# Catch Me If You Can opening titles — Kuntzel + Deygas (2002)

**Why it's in the set (classic): the canonical modern take on Saul Bass, where hand-made stamp silhouettes, flowing type and cool jazz make a chase through a flat, colour-coded 1960s world.**

## The idea
A con man and the "top man" chasing him run through a graphic world of airports, hotels, a hospital and a courtroom (*est.*, from the film's settings and stills); the hero is a silhouette because "the hero is a trickster", a character we don't know (*source*: Kuntzel + Deygas, Art of the Title). The characters are rubber stamps, made "from the same cutters as those used in the film by Frank Abagnale Jr." to forge documents (*source*).

## Structure and pacing
- Length not stated in the sources read (null). The film's opening runs about 2 minutes (*est.*, from memory of the film; unverified).
- A continuous chase in which the camera pans and tracks across flat sets; scenes change by colour field and by lines that become architecture (*est.*, from the stills on the studio page). Roughly 10–14 locations (*est.*).
- The colours "signal geographical and temporal transitions" (*source*).

## Typography
Credits are integrated into the set: names become signage, document fields and stamps, and lines of type slide in with the panning camera ("Flowing type, smooth lines", *source*: AotT). Layout and typography by Olivier Marquézy (*source*: credits). Faces: mid-century sans and slab lettering in white or black on colour fields (*est.*, from stills).

## Colour and light
A changing sequence of flat, saturated backgrounds (teal, orange, red, mustard, blue in the stills, *est.*), with black stamped silhouettes and white line work. It "slowly fad[es] to reveal the darkened truth", ending darker (*source*: AotT).

## Motion techniques
- Hand-carved rubber stamps animated "in a traditional manner on paper by hand"; the original stamps cut "in a few hours" are the final ones ("The magic of the first try was not altered", *source*).
- The hand-made animation was composited into "a precise, down-to-the-millimetre décor on a computer" (3D supervision by Robin Kobrynski) (*source*), so a crude texture lives inside a smooth camera.
- Silhouettes transform and swap (a pilot, a doctor, a lawyer), which echoes the plot (*est.*, from the film).

## Transitions
Lines that slide and turn into the next set; colour-field swaps; the camera tracking past a wall into the next room (*est.*, from stills and the AotT description "snaking and sneaking across the colorful jet-set world").

## Sound design and music
John Williams' main theme: a cool-jazz piece of alto sax, vibraphone and finger snaps over a walking bass. Kuntzel + Deygas: "Try another piece of music and you'll see it does not fit" (*source*). The animation is synced to it.

## What makes it world-class
1. A conceptual medium: stamps are forgery tools, so the technique is the story.
2. Imperfection held on purpose (ink voids, rough stamp edges) against a perfectly smooth, precisely composed camera.
3. Colour as a narrative device, with a new flat field for each place and time.
4. Type is part of the world (signs, papers), not laid on top.
5. Motion and edits phrase to the jazz, with clear swing, accents and pauses.

## What Anikit would need
`shape` and `image` silhouettes (stamped textures as images), `text` set into the scene, `camera` pans across a wide flat set, `matte` reveals and colour-field transitions. GAPs: a procedural rubber-stamp or ink material (voids, edge noise, per-frame variation); stepped "on twos" timing for hand-made layers inside smooth camera moves.

## Judge's checklist
- Does the technique itself say something about the subject (not just "flat style")?
- Are hand-made textures present and visible at 100% crop, while the camera and layout stay precise?
- Does each scene change of place come with a deliberate change of colour field?
- Are credits integrated into objects in the world rather than floating over it?
- Does the motion swing with the music (accents on the hits, pauses on the rests)?
