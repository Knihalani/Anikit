# FEUD: Bette and Joan main titles — Prologue / Kyle Cooper (2017)

**Why it's in the set: the best modern Saul Bass-lineage title. A single continuous shot of flat silhouettes and type in three flat colours, with every transition designed in camera.**

## The idea
Bette Davis and Joan Crawford, as tall, featureless, Giacometti-thin silhouettes, try to kill each other across five or six story beats. It cites *Baby Jane*, *Rear Window* and *Vertigo* (*source*: Art of the Title; motionpictures.org). Ryan Murphy asked for a retro look after Bass's *Anatomy of a Murder*; Cooper's brief to the team: "Make it consistent with the times" (*source*).

## Structure and pacing
- 76 s (*source*: Vimeo oEmbed).
- One continuous shot with no cuts (*source*: motionpictures.org). Instead of cuts, about 6–8 "beats" change through shape transformations, about 9–12 s each (*est.*, from the beat count in the source and the length).
- Named moments: a mogul's cigar ash becomes a shower of gold Oscar statuettes; Bette serves Joan a rat for dinner and both are pulled into a swirling vortex, which is taken from *Baby Jane* and signals madness (*source*).

## Typography
Credits set as part of the graphic composition, in condensed, mid-century display lettering that sits on the flat colour fields (*est.*, from stills; the typeface is not documented in the sources read). Names enter with the shapes rather than over them, in the manner of Bass.

## Colour and light
Flat yellow, red and blue backgrounds, with black and white silhouettes. Cooper reduced the palette late after Paul Rand's dictum "Too much color is no color at all"; the blue contains a touch of red and the red a touch of yellow, so the three harmonise (*source*). No gradients, no lighting.

## Motion techniques
- Imitates cel animation shot from above: limited, hand-made timing, restricted to effects possible in 1962 (*source*).
- Paper cut-out silhouettes (Matisse influence, *source*), with shapes that morph into each other to move the story on (*est.*).
- Gold Oscar statuettes fall as a cascade of repeated identical shapes (*source*).

## Transitions
All in camera: ash becomes statuettes; plates and objects become the next background; a spiral vortex swallows both women (*source*). No wipes or dissolves (one continuous shot, *source*).

## Sound design and music
Mac Quayle's main title theme (Emmy-nominated), a brassy, string-driven 1960s thriller pastiche; the animation is timed to the score (*source*).

## What makes it world-class
1. One continuous shot: every change of scene is a designed transformation of a shape, not a cut or a stock transition.
2. A disciplined three-colour palette tuned for harmony (each colour slightly tinted toward its neighbour), with large flat fields that read instantly.
3. Silhouettes act with posture alone. With no faces, the poses and proportions carry the character.
4. Each beat is a specific visual pun with a payoff (ash into Oscars, dinner into the vortex).
5. The animation respects its period: no motion blur, glow or 3D; the limited timing is intentional.

## What Anikit would need
`shape` paths for silhouettes, `text` credits, `path` and `morph`-style transitions between shapes, `matte`/`mask` reveals, `repeat` for the Oscar cascade, and `camera` for the continuous move. GAPs: an "on twos" stepped-timing option for a hand-drawn feel; authoring tools for a continuous one-shot in which shapes morph into the next scene's layout.

## Judge's checklist
- Are scene changes made by transforming shapes in frame, rather than by cuts or generic transitions?
- Is the palette limited to three or four flat colours that are visibly tuned together?
- Do silhouettes read clearly by pose alone, at any size?
- Does each beat have a concrete visual joke or metaphor with a payoff?
- Is the timing deliberately "handmade" (stepped, no smooth digital easing everywhere) when the style calls for it?
