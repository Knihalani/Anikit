# Hans Rosling's 200 Countries, 200 Years, 4 Minutes — BBC Four (2010) — all-time classic

**The most-cited data animation ever: one moving bubble chart and a presenter physically inside it, telling 200 years of health and wealth in one take.**

## The idea
Plot every country's life expectancy against income from 1810 to the present, bubble size = population, colour = region, and let time run while Rosling narrates and gestures at individual bubbles; the chart is composited as augmented reality into the space around him (*source*: coverage of the segment, e.g. mymodernmet/Brain Pickings titles; "120,000 numbers", *source*: search summaries of the BBC segment). Data from Gapminder.

Length: ~4 min (*source*: title); `length_s` null (no oEmbed duration). Analyse the 60 s that runs from the post-1948 surge to the present, the densest motion.

## Structure and pacing
- Effectively one continuous chart with the presenter; cuts between wider and closer framings of Rosling (*observed* hq2: a tight close-up of Rosling, dark background, a shaft of light at left).
- Pacing set by the year counter: decades pass in seconds, slowing for key events (world wars, the 1918 flu) — *est.* from Rosling's well-known delivery.

## Typography
Large year numeral behind the bubbles; axis labels (life expectancy, income per person on a log scale) — *est.* (Gapminder Trendalyzer conventions). Type is functional and sparse.

## Colour and light
Dark studio/warehouse space with motivated hard light (*observed*: hq2 light shaft); bubbles glow in saturated regional colours (Gapminder: Africa blue, Asia red, Europe orange/yellow, Americas green — *est.* from Gapminder convention).

## Motion techniques
Interpolated bubble motion between yearly data points; bubble growth for population; the presenter's gestures timed to bubble moves (*est.*).

## Transitions
Camera moves through the AR chart, cuts between presenter framings (*est.*).

## Sound design and music
VO-led: Rosling's energetic, sports-commentary delivery is the soundtrack; light music bed (*est.*).

## What makes it world-class
1. Four encodings at once (x, y, size, colour) plus time, and still instantly readable.
2. A human scale: the presenter in the chart turns data into a story with a guide.
3. Pacing tied to the data's time axis, slowing on the events that matter.
4. A single conclusion (the world got healthier and richer, unequally) that the motion itself proves.
